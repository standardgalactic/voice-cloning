My research frameworks. I am an independent researcher working across philosophy, mathematics,
physics, computer science, cognitive science, and systems architecture. My work is organized
around a central question. How do distinctions arise, persist, transform, and become admissible
within systems whose histories cannot simply be reversed? Rather than treating objects, identities,
observations, and decisions as independently given, I investigate the constraints and processes
through which they become distinguishable and operationally meaningful. Several interconnected
frameworks have emerged from this research. RSVP, Relativistic Scalar-Vector-Entropy Plenum.
My RSVP framework is an attempt to develop a constraint-first account of physical reality
using three interacting quantities, scalar capacity phi, vector flow v, and entropy
 S. Rather than beginning with particles moving through a pre-existing spacetime, I investigate
how physical structure, motion, and temporal ordering might emerge from the dynamics of
a continuous plenum. A central proposition is that time should be understood through irreversible
transformation and entropy production, rather than assumed to be an independent background
parameter. RSVP remains a theoretical research program, with mathematical consistency, physical
interpretation, and empirical adequacy requiring separate demonstrations.
Spherepop Calculus and Spherepop OS. Spherepop is my framework for understanding computation,
identity, and agency through admissible state transitions and irreversible histories. Its principal
operations include POP, REFUSE, BIND, TRANSFORM, VERIFY, and COLLAPSE. I treat identity as something
established through a history of transformations rather than a static label. The computational
architecture emphasizes append-only records, deterministic replay, explicit refusal, provenance,
and verifiable commitments. Spherepop OS extends these principles into an operating system
architecture, in which actions must satisfy specified constraints before becoming committed
state. Its witness plane records evidence of transitions, without confusing the record of an action with
the action itself.
MEM8, Resonant Memory and Reconstruction. MEM8 is my experimental architecture for for representing memory through compact, resonant state structures.
It investigates how retrieval can emerge from phase relationships, interference, contextual constraints, and attractor
dynamics, rather than straightforward retrieval of stored representations. The framework includes an eight-byte memory-cell
representation, phase addressing, interference gating, and observer-relative reconstruction.
I am interested in memory as an active process in which present conditions participate in determining which aspects of a prior state
become recoverable. This connects my computational experiments with broader questions about human recollection,
forgetting, and the reconstruction of experience.
CLIO, HYDRA, and TARTAN. These frameworks address different aspects of coordinated computation. I know this as of how much
CLIO organizes operations through a structured sequence of hypothesis, derivation, action, witnessing, and commitment.
HYDRA investigates scheduling and coordination among concurrent processes, while TARTAN develops a tiling-based
architecture in which local states interact through constrained relationships and memory kernels.
Together, they support my broader investigation of how local operations can produce globally coherent behavior,
without assuming that every component possesses a complete representation of the system.
Admissibility and Irreversibility
Admissibility is the unifying philosophical and mathematical concern of much of my work.
I distinguish between what can be described, what can be constructed, what can be verified,
and what can legitimately be committed as an action.
I investigate agency through the selection of possible histories under constraints,
especially where decisions produce consequences that cannot be completely undone.
My work on the Reversibility Gate and the Latency of Evidence extends this approach to decisions under uncertainty.
I distinguish the time at which evidence is observed, the time at which a claim becomes decidable,
and the time at which action becomes justified.
I also examine how the maturity of evidence and the reversibility of an intervention
affect the conditions under which action should proceed.
Distinction, Continuity, and Reconstruction
Another major research direction concerns the difference between apparent continuity and demonstrable continuity.
I investigate how systems preserve identity through change,
how representations can conceal discontinuities,
and why the existence of a plausible reconstruction does not establish the preservation of an original state.
This work extends into model compression, machine learning, epistemology, software verification,
and theories of consciousness.
